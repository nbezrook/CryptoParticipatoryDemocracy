Resource constraints, iterative coordination, and the limits of feasibility

A reproducible simulation studyModel 1.0.06 councils · 120 members · 3 cooperatives

Abstract

We examine a simplified, one-period model of participatory economic planning. Consumer councils submit budget-constrained proposals for vegetables, bread, and electricity. Indicative prices respond to scarce resources, while a separate proportional allocation rule enforces physical capacity limits. The interactive experiment below illustrates how supply disruptions affect proposal revision, consumption, and convergence. A feasible plan may emerge through reduced consumption; convergence alone does not establish that basic needs are met.

Keywords: participatory planning; cooperative economies; resource allocation; reproducible simulation

01

Model and allocation procedure

The federation contains a farm, a bakery, an energy cooperative, and six consumer councils. Fixed production recipes connect final goods to four resource constraints: farm labor, bakery labor, shared electricity, and harvested grain. Each member receives an equal credit entitlement; seeded preference shares differ between councils.

The procedure draws on the idea of iterative proposal revision in participatory economics [1, 2]. It is a reduced model of resource accounting, rather than a complete implementation of its institutions.

For council c and good g, proposals follow Cobb–Douglas budget demand. The indicative price combines a fixed base cost with resource-specific scarcity premiums:

qcg = Bc wcgpgpg = bg + ∑r arg λr(1)

Here, B is the credit budget, w the preference share, a the resource requirement, and λ a nonnegative scarcity premium. Premiums rise when a resource is overrequested and fall when capacity is underused. At every round, proposals are scaled by a common factor to produce a feasible provisional allocation:

α = min (1, minr KrDr)xcg = α qcg(2)

Stopping criterion. Here, Kr is resource capacity and Dr total requested use. Convergence requires excess resource demand and the active scarcity-premium residuals to be within 0.8%. Proportional rationing enforces feasibility independently of this criterion.

04

Discussion and limitations

Feasibility and consumption. Higher indicative prices can reduce proposals until the resource constraints are satisfied. The allocation and initial-basket indices must therefore be interpreted alongside convergence: resource balance alone is not a welfare result.

Institutional scope. The model assumes fixed production recipes, divisible goods, truthful capacity reports, and fixed preferences. It excludes inventories, investment, strategic reporting, council voting, and need-based entitlements. Computational planning has a wider theoretical literature [3, 4]; this experiment evaluates a limited numerical procedure.

Further work. Existing planning prototypes and distributed resource vocabularies provide useful precedents [5, 6]. Extensions could compare rationing rules, model uncertain production, and test verifiable resource commitments. Cryptographic proofs and private budgets are not implemented in this study.

Appendix A. Update rule and reproducibility

Scarcity premiums are initialized at zero and updated after each recorded round using a projected feedback step:

λrt+1 = max (0, λrt + η (Drt / Kr − 1))(A1)

For resources with a positive premium, the residual is the absolute deviation from full utilization; otherwise, it is excess demand only. The process stops when the maximum residual is at most 0.008 or the update limit is reached. Round zero precedes the first update.
